An event registration funnel that fills every seat has three parts working together: one focused landing page that makes registering the obvious next step, tightly targeted ads that reach only the people the event is for, and a follow-up sequence that turns registrations into actual attendance. Most events that struggle do not have a traffic problem; they have a trust problem on the page or a drop-off problem between registration and the day itself. This article walks through the structure we used for SLP Quest, a niche medical education event in Jaipur that achieved a 95 percent registration rate, 500 qualified leads and 1.5 times the footfall of its previous event.

The challenge with niche events

SLP Quest, a division of Insight Rehabilitation, wanted to host an event for speech-language pathologists in Jaipur. The audience was small, spread across the region and largely unaware of the organisation. That combination is common for professional events, workshops, CME sessions and B2B summits: broad advertising wastes money, and the few people who are relevant need a strong reason to trust an unfamiliar organiser.

The same principles apply to exhibitions, product launches and webinars, even when the audience is broader.

The landing page: one page, one action

The single most important decision is to send traffic to a dedicated registration page, not your homepage or a general events listing. Every element on that page should either build trust or move the visitor towards registering.

What to include above the fold

  • A headline that names the audience and the benefit, not just the event name.
  • Date, city, venue and duration, visible without scrolling.
  • A short registration form with only essential fields: name, phone, email and one qualifying field such as profession or organisation.
  • A clear button with a specific label such as "Reserve my seat".

What builds trust below

  • Speaker names, photos and credentials.
  • A session-by-session agenda with timings.
  • Photos or short clips from earlier editions, and testimonials from past attendees.
  • Accreditation, certificates or credits offered, if any.
  • Venue details with a map link, parking and nearest landmarks.
  • A short FAQ covering fees, refunds, what to bring and who should attend.

Remove the main site navigation from the page. Every extra link is a way to leave without registering. Make sure the page loads quickly on mobile, since most visitors from social ads will be on a phone.

Targeting: reach only the right people

For a niche audience, precise targeting keeps cost per acquisition low. Broad campaigns might produce cheap clicks, but most of them will never register.

  1. Start with interest and job targeting on Meta and LinkedIn: profession, related interests, relevant associations and publications.
  2. Layer geography carefully. Target the host city and the surrounding cities from which people realistically travel.
  3. Upload existing contact lists from past attendees or enquiries as custom audiences, and build lookalikes from them.
  4. Retarget page visitors who did not register with reminders featuring speakers, agenda highlights and seat availability.
  5. Use search ads for specific terms if people look for workshops or courses in your topic.

Refresh creative every one to two weeks. For a small audience, the same ad shown too often becomes invisible.

Follow-up: turn registrations into attendance

A registration is a promise, not a guarantee. Without follow-up, a significant share of free registrants will not attend. A simple sequence closes the gap.

Key takeaway

Filling seats is a trust and follow-up problem more than a traffic problem. Build one focused page with visible proof, target only the professionals the event is for, and run a reminder sequence from registration to the morning of the event.

A practical campaign timeline

A funnel works best when each stage has time to do its job. For a local professional event, a workable plan looks like this:

  1. Six weeks out: finalise speakers, agenda and venue, then build and test the landing page.
  2. Five weeks out: launch awareness ads to the core audience and email past attendees with an early-bird offer.
  3. Three weeks out: shift budget towards retargeting page visitors and the best-performing audiences.
  4. Final week: run "last seats" reminders, pause cold campaigns and focus on confirming attendance.

Keep a small reserve budget for the last ten days. Registrations often rise sharply close to the date, and that is when retargeting is cheapest per registration.

Metrics to watch during the campaign

  • Landing page conversion rate: the share of visitors who register. If it is low, fix the page before spending more on ads.
  • Cost per registration by channel and audience.
  • Lead quality: the share of registrants who match your target profile.
  • Attendance rate: registrants who actually turn up, tracked at check-in.

Track each registration's source using UTM tags so you know which audiences brought people who attended, not just people who signed up. That data makes the next event cheaper to fill.

Frequently asked questions

How early should we start promoting an event?

For a local professional event, four to six weeks is usually enough. Larger conferences or events that need travel should start eight to twelve weeks ahead, with an early-bird offer to create an initial push.

Should registration be free or paid?

Free events get more registrations but lower attendance. Even a small fee or a refundable deposit tends to improve turn-up rates. If you keep it free, invest more in reminders.

Which ad platform works best for event registrations?

Meta usually delivers the most registrations for consumer and many professional events. LinkedIn suits senior B2B audiences but costs more per registration. Search ads help when people actively look for workshops in your topic.
